The best alarm is the one the sleeper can perceive. A louder ringtone is not automatically that cue. Some people wake to vibration, some to a specific sound, some to a light in the room, and some need more than the phone. This page is about the settings Apple documents, and about the gap those settings do not close.

It is not medical advice and not a hearing assessment. If you miss alarms in a way that affects safety, talk with a qualified clinician and consider a dedicated alerting device.

## Start with the Clock sound

Apple's alarm article says Do Not Disturb, the Ring/Silent switch, and Silent mode do not mute the sound of a built-in Clock alarm. If a Clock alarm is quiet or only vibrates, check two things: Ringtone and Alerts volume, and that the alarm sound is not None. You can turn on Change with Buttons so the volume buttons adjust that alert volume.

Headphones do not replace the speaker. Apple says that when headphones are connected, the alarm still plays at a set volume through the iPhone's built-in speakers as well as through wired and wireless headphones. Take the headphones off the nightstand test anyway. A buried phone under a pillow is a different problem than a low slider.

## Haptics are not the whole alarm

Sounds & Haptics controls vibration patterns for ringtones and alerts. Apple's guide for those settings is about calls, texts, and similar alerts. Do not assume a custom vibration you made for texts is the Clock alarm. In Clock, pick a sound on purpose. If the only feedback you feel is a weak buzz, the sound is probably None or the phone is in StandBy.

Apple says that when you use StandBy, haptics for alarms are disabled. If the phone charges on its side as a bedside clock, you need an audible sound. A Watch tap can add a wrist cue for the person wearing it. See [Apple Watch and shared wake routines](/blog/apple-watch-and-shared-wake-routines).

## Flash for Alerts is a notification setting

Apple documents LED Flash and screen flash for notifications: Settings, Accessibility, Audio & Visual, Flash for Alerts. The support article describes this as a blink when you receive a notification, useful if you might miss audible alerts. It works when the device is locked, with options for flashing while unlocked and in Silent mode.

That document is about notifications. It does not say Clock alarms flash the LED. Treat flash as a help for calls and alerts you might miss while awake, and test a Clock alarm separately. If the flash does not blink when the alarm fires, do not build the morning around it.

## Two people, two cue sets

A shared time does not mean a shared sense. One person may need a sharp tone and a Watch. The other may need a lower sound because a baby or a roommate is asleep, plus a wrist tap. Write the cue next to the wake window in the [shared wake agreement](/blog/shared-wake-agreement). "Same alarm" can still mean different sounds.

Test in the daytime, in the spot where the phone will actually spend the night. A test at the kitchen table does not tell you what a pillow does to the speaker.

## When the phone is the wrong tool

If someone regularly sleeps through the phone, add a backup that does not depend on that speaker: a Watch, a bedside alarm with a bed shaker, or another person who is already awake and has agreed to be the fallback. Do not make the other sleeper the only backup. That trades one access problem for a household fight.

## FAQ

### Does Silent mode mute my iPhone alarm?

Apple says Silent mode and Do Not Disturb do not affect the sound of built-in Clock alarms. Third-party notifications can still be quiet.

### Will the camera flash blink for a Clock alarm?

Apple documents LED and screen flash for notifications, not as a Clock alarm feature. Test it. If it does not flash with the alarm, use sound or a Watch.

### Can roommates use different alarm sounds for the same wake time?

Yes. The shared plan is the time and the rule. The cue can be personal.
